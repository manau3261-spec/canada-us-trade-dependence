# Design A · U.S. trade exposure and export performance

**Status.** A conditional, product-level study of the March 2025 U.S. Section 232 steel tariff. The design can be run as a descriptive event comparison if the legal mapping passes and the identification checks fail. Its causal interpretation would require additional evidence.

1. **Research question.** How did the relative performance of Canadian domestic exports of covered steel products to the U.S. change around the March 12, 2025 tariff, compared with the same products in other markets and comparable products without that additional Section 232 duty?
2. **Mechanism.** A higher landed cost can lower U.S. orders, compress exporters’ margins, trigger front-loading or redirect shipments. Delivery contracts and rules of origin can delay the response. Third-market redirection is itself a possible response.
3. **Hypothesis.** Covered products experience weaker U.S.-bound export value relative to the comparison destinations after implementation. Anticipation may generate positive leads. Shipment quantities could decline even if nominal export values do not.
4. **Dependent variable.** Monthly Canadian domestic export value in current CAD for a product–destination pair, including verified zero flows. Estimate with Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML) in levels. Estimate the physical-quantity analogue only within a stable HS8 line and quantity unit with adequate coverage. Canadian shipment dates and U.S. entry dates can differ.
5. **Main explanatory variables.** Interact the U.S. destination indicator, a pre-audited Section 232 steel treatment indicator and the post indicator. Code products as treated only where the covered U.S. variants map cleanly to the Canadian HS6 and the duty applies to full value. Comparison HS6 groups must pass an overlapping-policy and concordance audit. February 10 is the announcement and March 12 the effective date. Use April–May 2025 as the cleanest available full-month post window before the June increase, omit the mixed March month in the main contrast, and inspect February as anticipation. Use only January 2025 and earlier to assess unaffected pre-periods.
6. **Controls.** The fixed effects absorb product-wide price/supply changes and destination-wide demand or exchange-rate changes common across products. Pre-audit CUSMA usage, anti-dumping/countervailing orders, quotas and concurrent changes. Use a restricted stable-policy comparison or predetermined interactions where identifiable. Never control for post-policy import preference uptake, duties paid, or volumes as if these were exogenous.
7. **Unit.** Canadian HS6 domestic export product × destination × calendar month. National origin removes sparse province splits. Canada’s HS8 suffixes and the U.S. HTS10 suffixes are domestic classifications and cannot be equated. A six-digit match is eligible only where entry treatment remains homogeneous across material U.S. child lines.
8. **Window.** January 2022–May 2025. Use the U.S. and a short list of other markets selected on pre-period trade, reporting stability and policy overlap before looking at outcomes. Select nearby uncovered metal products on availability and comparable pre-history, excluding directly competing products if substitution would compromise the comparison.
9. **Sources.** Statistics Canada CIMT annual reference-year files of monthly HS8 domestic exports. Phase 4A verified 2024–2025 archives. Acquire and audit 2022–2023 before estimation. Verify 2025 revisions of HTSUS Chapter 99, the legal annex and CBP entry guidance. USITC DataWeb imports for consumption, Canada origin, supplies customs value and calculated duties for validation where available. [CIMT open data](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/2909a648-5753-4924-878a-b069392d9cde) · [Steel proclamation](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/adjusting-imports-of-steel-into-the-united-states/) · [HTS editions](https://hts.usitc.gov/download/archive) · [USITC DataWeb](https://www.usitc.gov/applications/dataweb) · [Legal review](tariff_policy_timeline.md)
10. **Equation.** For exports `V[h,d,m]`, specify `E[V[h,d,m] | .] = exp(a[h,d] + b[h,m] + c[d,m] + beta * US[d] * Covered[h] * Post[m])`. In an event-time version replace the post term with relative-month interactions, omit January 2025, and label transition months. `exp(beta) − 1` is a proportional change in the conditional mean for the multiplicative contrast. [PPML reference, Santos Silva and Tenreyro](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/tenreyro/lgw.html)
11. **Fixed effects.** HS6–destination, HS6–month and destination–month. Check that treatment remains identified after zero-only and separated groups are excluded. If a treatment label is absorbed or a legal cohort has no support, record the loss explicitly.
12. **Inference.** Cluster by a stable pre-specified HS4 or legal product-family grouping to account for serial and cross-flow dependence. Report the number of independent families, the treated-family count and effective leverage. Conditional on adequate support, compare conventional clustered intervals with studentized restricted wild-cluster inference suitable for PPML scores. Single-cohort and concentration risks can make both unreliable. When the pilot yields few independent families or no defensible inference, provide event paths and sensitivity ranges as descriptive evidence. A placebo-date distribution is diagnostic.
13. **Identification assumptions.** Without the steel action, covered and uncovered products would have followed comparable U.S.–other-market paths after the fixed effects. Anticipation must be delimited. Composition, origin compliance, inventory timing and other policy changes must have no differential residual impact. A redirection effect changes the comparison-market outcome and narrows the estimand to relative performance. A claim about direct U.S. demand requires a credible non-spillover comparison.
14. **Confounding.** The March 4–7 border tariff and preference pivot, April reciprocal tariffs on other countries, Canadian retaliation, U.S. demand, steel prices, transport delays, quota changes and trade remedies may all affect the comparison. Controls with changing non-USMCA incidence are problematic even with destination–month effects. U.S. imports and Canadian exports can diverge on valuation, origin attribution and month of entry.
15. **Sample and missingness.** An illustrative pre-screen with 40 HS6 groups and six destinations yields 9,840 product–destination–month slots across 41 months, or 9,600 after omitting March 2025. This is design arithmetic. The number of eligible tariff codes, observed zeros and unsupported groups remains unverified, and varying counts are reported before PPML. No row can be called a zero until official coverage and the no-flow convention are validated. Suppressed, unresolved splits and partial coverage remain flagged. The two clean post months limit dynamic precision.
16. **Robustness.** Inspect leads and placebo dates, exclude likely diversion destinations, leave one product family or destination out, apply a strict no-antidumping and no-overlap sample, use pre-period weights as a secondary aggregate, change the start month only as pre-registered sensitivity, and compare U.S. mirrors and stable-unit quantities. Report alternative margin outcomes such as active product–destination pairs and unweighted vs value-weighted trade effects, with explicit estimands.
17. **Interpretation.** A limited policy-period contrast for mapped steel lines, potentially causal only if the counterfactual and timing checks pass. It cannot identify the average impact of all U.S. tariffs, a national export change or who bears tariff costs.

**Go/no-go.** Approve the legal-line and concordance audit before choosing comparison groups. Allow tariff estimation only after an adequate comparison, pre-period, post window and independent treatment-family count are demonstrated. [Model selection](model_selection.md)

## Synthèse en français

Comparer les exportations mensuelles canadiennes d’acier vers les États-Unis et d’autres destinations autour du 12 mars 2025, avec des produits comparables non visés. Vérifier d’abord les codes et exonérations, l’exposition aux autres mesures et la qualité des comparateurs. Le résultat porterait sur la performance relative des produits retenus.
